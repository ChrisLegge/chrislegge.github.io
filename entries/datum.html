Datum

July 2026 – Present

An iPad app built solo, from first sketch to App Store submission.

Privacy Policy

Overview

Datum is an engineering project notebook for iPad. It exists because my own projects were scattered across sketchbooks, spreadsheets, camera rolls and half-finished CAD files — and by the time I came back to a build six months later, the reasoning behind every decision had gone with them.

The app keeps a whole project in one place: ideas, Apple Pencil sketches, circuit schematics, CAD models, parts and budget, cutting lists, test plans, photos and notes. I designed and built it solo in SwiftUI and SwiftData, targeting iPadOS 17 and up.

Sketching with PencilKit

The sketch canvas is built on PencilKit over a 12000 × 9000pt sheet, with thirteen brushes, square, isometric and dot grids, and a measuring tool that reads real millimetres once you calibrate the drawing scale against a known length.

Because technical drawing needs straight lines, I added line-lock modes that replace a freehand stroke with a clean line — optionally snapped to 15° increments or to grid intersections — and shape tools that turn a roughed-out circle or hexagon into a precise one. The paper, grain and grid are drawn into a CATiledLayer inside the canvas so they pan and zoom in lockstep with the ink rather than sliding independently.

Schematic Capture & Rule Checking

The circuit section is a schematic editor I wrote from scratch — every component symbol is drawn with SwiftUI's Canvas rather than shipped as artwork, so they stay sharp at any zoom. Components are placed, dragged and wired pin-to-pin on an infinite grid.

You can define your own parts in a reusable component library: give an ESP32 a name, notes and labelled pins on any of the four sides, mark which pins are mandatory, and it renders as a proper labelled box. A rule checker then flags floating pins, missing ground or supply, direct shorts and duplicate references — and any required pin left unconnected is raised as an error. The finished schematic exports as a netlist, or rolls straight into the project's parts list.

STL Viewing

Datum imports and renders STL models in SceneKit, using a binary and ASCII parser I wrote by hand. Models are auto-centred, scaled to fit and framed by the camera, with a wireframe toggle for checking geometry.

Writing the parser taught me a lesson about memory alignment the hard way: a binary STL triangle record is 50 bytes, so every other triangle starts on a two-byte boundary and a naive aligned read traps instantly. Switching to unaligned loads and parsing off the main thread turned a guaranteed crash into a smooth import.

Build, Budget & Testing

The build section covers the unglamorous half of a project: a parts list with suppliers and prices, a budget broken down by category using Swift Charts, a cutting list for pieces to cut, mill, drill or print, and an ordered build checklist. A work timer logs hours against phases so a finished project can tell you what it really cost in both money and time.

Testing is deliberately structured: define what a pass looks like before you run it, record what actually happened, and keep a lessons-learned log of what broke and why. That log is searchable across every project I've ever built, which is the part I use most — the mistakes turn out to be far more reusable than the successes.

Engineering Calculator

Rather than reaching for a phone mid-build, Datum has around forty engineering formulas built in — Ohm's law, voltage dividers, LED series resistors, RC and LC networks, gear ratios, cantilever deflection, stepper steps per millimetre, feed and speed rates and more. Tapping any field solves for that variable instead.

On top of that I wrote a small recursive-descent expression parser, which powers a grapher with drag-to-trace, numeric differentiation and integration, root finding, and solvers for simultaneous equations and quadratics. Any result can be dropped straight into the project as a note so the working is recorded alongside the build.

What I Learned

Datum is the largest solo Swift codebase I've written — around 9,000 lines across SwiftUI, SwiftData, PencilKit, SceneKit, PDFKit and Swift Charts. Most of what I learned came from bugs that only appear on real hardware: unaligned memory reads crashing the STL parser, SwiftData mutations happening off the main actor, and PencilKit silently rewriting black ink to white because the app forces dark mode and it assumes a dark canvas.

That last one took three attempts to fix properly, because each time I verified it I was measuring the wrong thing. It was a good reminder that a fix isn't finished until you've proven it on the path the user actually takes — not the one that was easiest to test.
