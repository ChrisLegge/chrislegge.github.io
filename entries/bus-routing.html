Predictive Bus Routing

Engineers Without Borders Design Challenge · March 2026

Grand Finalist, top 36 in an international competition. I was responsible for all of the hardware.

Overview

Ladywood is a ward in Birmingham that sits in the most deprived 5% of areas in England, and a lot of the people who live there don't have a car. The buses run to a fixed timetable, so they can't react when demand changes with the time of day, the weather or an event in the city.

Our team of four built a system that predicts how many people will be waiting at each of 15 bus stops and then reroutes the buses to match. Two of my teammates built an XGBoost model to predict demand and a routing algorithm to plan the routes, and another built a Unity simulation. My job was the hardware: a physical, to scale LED map of the Ladywood bus network that shows the routes and demand live, so you can see what the system is doing without a screen.

Building the Map

The map is 156 WS2812B addressable LEDs laid out on an acrylic and foam panel at roughly 1:8000 scale. Every bus stop is a light and every road between stops is a lit strip, 15 stops joined by 22 strip segments in total. I traced the road network from a map of the ward, cut the strips to length and soldered every joint between them by hand so the whole thing runs as one long chain.

I originally designed a custom PCB for the map, but getting it made was well outside our budget and would have been a big risk to the schedule, so I went with LED strips instead. They do the same job and meant I could get the full map working much sooner.

Before writing any real control logic I used an Arduino to bring the hardware up. I ran a colour sweep down every segment to check that no LEDs were dead and that every solder joint was passing the data signal cleanly, lit up individual stops to check the layout matched the real network, and worked out where to inject 5V power and measured the current draw. By January all 156 LEDs were individually addressable.

Why the Arduino Wasn't Enough

WS2812B LEDs are very fussy about timing. Each bit is sent as a pulse: a 1 is 900 ns high then 360 ns low, a 0 is 360 ns high then 900 ns low, and there is only about 150 ns of tolerance either side. On an Arduino Uno running at 16 MHz this has to be bit banged in software with interrupts switched off for the whole transmission, and one glitch corrupts the whole strip.

The bigger problem was memory. The Uno only has 2 KB of SRAM, which isn't enough to store the route tables for every scenario, so it could only ever display colours that a laptop sent to it over serial. I wanted the map to run on its own, so even though nobody on the team had used one before, I moved the design onto an FPGA.

The FPGA Design

The board is a Terasic DE1-SoC with an Intel Cyclone V FPGA and a 50 MHz clock. I only knew the basics of Verilog going in and taught myself enough in about ten days to get a working WS2812B driver, then built the rest of the design on top of it in Quartus Prime.

The LED driver is a state machine that counts clock cycles to generate each pulse. At 50 MHz one cycle is 20 ns, so a 0 bit is 18 cycles high and 45 low, a 1 bit is 45 high and 18 low, and a reset is held low for 3000 cycles. Because it is done in hardware there is no jitter at all, and the whole map of 156 LEDs refreshes around 210 times a second.

On top of the driver I wrote:

  • A ROM holding the model's output for 4 weather scenarios across 8 time slots of the day, which I generated straight from the model's route plan with a Python script
  • 53 road paths between stops, worked out with a breadth first search, so buses follow the real roads instead of jumping from stop to stop
  • Three bus state machines that switch between dwelling at a stop for about 1.9 seconds and travelling one LED every 96 ms. Two buses follow the routes the model picks and the third tours all 15 stops
  • Switch controls to pick the scenario and time slot, and a demo mode that steps through every time slot every 30 seconds
  • A two flip flop synchroniser on the reset button so it can't cause metastability

The Grand Finals Setup

For the grand finals I split the demo across three devices so it could show live data and still be completely reliable on the day. The FPGA holds the demand ROM and the switches pick the scenario. It talks to a Raspberry Pi 5 over UART at 9600 baud using 17 byte frames, each one starting with a sync byte and ending with an XOR checksum so a corrupted frame gets thrown away. I wrote the UART receiver, transmitter and frame parser for the FPGA in SystemVerilog.

The Pi simulates the three buses moving around their routes, sends their positions back to the FPGA to show on the seven segment displays, and broadcasts everything over WiFi to an Arduino Uno R4 that drives the LED strip and pulses the busiest stop. The Pi runs its own hotspot and serves a web dashboard, so the whole thing works with no internet and no laptop.

What I Would Do Differently

The design works on the board, but when I went back through the Quartus timing report it doesn't meet timing. The worst path misses its setup time by 11.24 ns. The colour for each LED was being worked out every single clock cycle through a long chain of ROM lookups and a big priority encoder, and that logic is too deep to settle inside one 20 ns clock period.

Each LED takes about 1,500 clock cycles to send, so there is no reason to calculate its colour every cycle. The fix is to work the colour out once, register it, and then shift it out. It would also let me pipeline the lookups properly. It is the most useful thing I took from the project: getting a design to work on the bench and getting it to be correct are not the same.

I would also like to go back to the custom PCB now I know more, as it would make the map far neater and easier to scale up to more stops.

Results

Out of hundreds of entries, our team was selected as one of 36 Grand Finalists in the Engineers Without Borders Design Challenge. As well as the map, I made a 3D animated visualisation of the Ladywood network in Blender for our presentation, tracing the roads and canals and modelling a bus and a bus stop.

We planned the whole project with a Gantt chart, split the work by what each of us was best at and held a short weekly check in just for anything that was blocked. That check in made a big difference, and it is something I will keep doing on group projects.
